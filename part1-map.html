Asthma Alley After the Toll: The Map

Part 1 mapping model. What happened to traffic and air across New York City after congestion pricing started on January 5, 2025, and who lives where it got worse.

For CUNY Datathon 2026, Reimagining NYC Data through August 2026

The short version. About 11% fewer vehicles now enter the toll zone. Air across the city barely changed, and the Health Department found no statistically significant change at any of its highway monitors. The increases other studies report are next to highways that drivers use to get around the toll, and several of those highways run through the neighborhoods with the city's highest asthma rates.

In 2025, all 15 of the highest-asthma districts did worse than the city on NO₂. Eight of them are in areas getting the MTA's mitigation money. The other seven get none. Every layer on this map is here so you can check these claims yourself.

Is the pattern new?

One bad year for high-asthma districts could be noise. So we asked the same question for every year since 2021: did districts with more asthma, or more poverty, tend to do worse than the city? Each bar is a rank correlation between −1 and +1. Zero means no pattern.

For NO₂, 2025 is the first year with a clear yes: +0.52 for asthma and +0.55 for poverty. In the three years before, the correlation was near zero or pointed the other way. Still, swings this size have happened before. In 2022 to 2023 it was −0.44. PM2.5 shows no pattern in 2025, and black carbon, which comes largely from diesel engines, leaned slightly the other way. We read the NO₂ result as a reason to measure these neighborhoods more closely, not as proof that the toll caused it.

What the city can do with this

Measure where the money isn't

Seven flagged districts get none of the MTA's $105 million in place-based mitigation: Central Harlem, Kingsbridge Heights, Bedford-Stuyvesant, East New York, Brownsville, East Flatbush and the Rockaways. Brownsville ranks second on our priority index. The Brooklyn and Queens districts are far from the routes drivers use to avoid the toll, so the toll may not explain their NO₂. That is the case for monitors before money. South Bronx Unite showed that residents can run a dense low-cost sensor network, and the same could work here.

Build the corridor with money that already exists

Our Part 2 design for Bruckner Boulevard in Mott Haven costs an estimated $10.1 to $19.3 million. The MTA has allocated $24.0 million to Hunts Point and Mott Haven, and its program already pays for roadside vegetation and park upgrades. The corridor is that kind of project, in the district that ranks first on our priority index, which makes it a natural fit for money that is already set aside.

Rerun it every year

Every input is public, and the air data come from the Health Department's public GitHub repository. When the 2026 NYCCAS estimates come out, rerunning our scripts updates every layer. That matters because the car toll goes up to $12 in 2028 and $15 in 2031. Each increase could push more drivers onto the bypass highways, and this map can show whether it does.

The four questions

1. Did congestion pricing reduce air pollution and traffic, or does the 22% hide a mixed picture?

Traffic went down: about 11% fewer vehicles entered the zone. Air across the city barely moved. Annual PM2.5 was 6.26 µg/m³ in 2024 and 6.29 in 2025. The 22% comes from daily peak readings inside the zone over about five months, so it describes the zone's worst hours, not the city's year. At the highway monitors, PM2.5 fell at most sites and times of day. The exception is the Trans-Manhattan site, where it rose at every hour even though traffic there barely changed.

2. Is pollution being eliminated or shifted, and toward vulnerable communities?

Mostly the changes are small, and some traffic moved. The toll-free FDR Drive gained 8.9%, and truck crossings on the Bronx-Queens bridges rose 2% to 4%. The increases other studies found are next to these bypass routes. The clearest overlap with vulnerable neighborhoods is in NO₂, the pollutant most tied to traffic. In 2025, all 15 districts in the top quarter for adult asthma emergency visits did worse than the city on NO₂, by 3.6 points on average compared with 1.0 for the rest. In the three years before, 3 to 10 of the 15 did. Eight of the 15 are in MTA mitigation areas and seven are not.

3. What do the South Bronx monitors show that the citywide average hides?

South Bronx Unite's 19 community sensors found PM2.5 up 0.22 µg/m³ on average. Thirteen sensors went up and two went down significantly. Four next to expressways rose by as much as 1.29. The Health Department's Major Deegan site in Mott Haven points the same way (PM2.5 +0.59, NO +3.48 ppb), but the change is not statistically significant, and its time-of-day data show PM2.5 slightly lower at every hour with traffic down 4% to 5%. In NYCCAS, Bronx NO₂ rose 4.4 points more than the city, the largest Bronx gap in four years, though swings that size happen elsewhere in normal years.

4. What if the BQE or Major Deegan were removed or turned into a boulevard?

The Deegan carried 128,727 vehicles a day in 2025, and the BQE about 142,000. Studies of road closures find that about 11% to 22% of traffic disappears when road space is cut. A four-lane boulevard carries roughly 40,000 to 50,000 vehicles a day. That leaves 50,000 to 75,000 vehicles a day from the Deegan looking for another route, most likely the Harlem River Drive through East Harlem, and 60,000 to 90,000 from the BQE on Williamsburg streets. Both would move pollution into other high-asthma neighborhoods. Traffic has to drop first, and removal can follow.

How we built it

Air quality. NYCCAS annual averages by community district from the Health Department's Environment and Health Data Portal, 2021 to 2025. "Change vs city" is a district's percent change minus the citywide percent change, in percentage points. We ran the same comparison for each year before the toll to see how much districts move in a normal year. Monitor results come from the Health Department's August 2026 evaluation, Goldberg, Anand and Westervelt (preprint, 2025) and South Bronx Unite (study summary, May 2026).

Traffic. MTA hourly crossings summed for February to July and for full years, then converted to daily averages. Highway counts at ten sites come from NYS DOT and NYC DOT, as published in the Health Department report's appendix. The truck route around Manhattan is drawn roughly, for illustration.

Vulnerability. Adult asthma emergency visits (age-adjusted, 2023) and the share of residents below the poverty line (American Community Survey 2015 to 2019), both from the same portal. "Is the pattern new?" uses Spearman rank correlations across all 59 districts.

Toll zone and mitigation. Boundary from the Health Department report's map, with toll-free roads traced along its east and west edges. Rates and the $105 million place-based mitigation program come from the MTA. The MTA lists mitigation money by neighborhood, so we matched neighborhoods to community districts by area overlap. Treat the match as approximate.

Limits. NYCCAS district values are model estimates, and the real-time monitors are less precise than filter monitors. None of these data can prove the toll caused a local change on its own. The Health Department's control-site analysis is the strongest evidence, and it found no significant change.
